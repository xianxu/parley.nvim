### The setup

Io orbits Jupiter every 42.5 hours and disappears into Jupiter's shadow each time. That's a natural clock, visible from Earth, ticking ~206 times a year. Rømer had years of eclipse timings from the Paris Observatory (many taken by Cassini, his boss).

The critical geometry: Earth and Jupiter orbit at different rates, so the Earth–Jupiter distance oscillates over the ~399-day synodic[^r-mer-delay] period, by up to 2 AU (Earth's orbital diameter).

### What Rømer noticed

Eclipses did not arrive on schedule, and the error was **not random** — it tracked Earth's orbital phase:

- When Earth was moving *away* from Jupiter, successive eclipses ran progressively **late**.
- When Earth was moving *toward* Jupiter, they ran progressively **early**.
- The discrepancy accumulated to roughly a quarter hour over half a synodic cycle, then unwound.


🌿: 2026-09-08.14-51-53.464.md: Rømer's light-speed eclipse timing

This is the whole argument. If light were instantaneous, the eclipse of a distant clock would be seen exactly when it happened, regardless of distance. A distance-correlated timing residual means the signal takes time to cross the gap.

His famous demonstration: in September 1676 he announced that the eclipse predicted for 9 November would occur about 10 minutes late. It did.

### The epistemically clean part

Notice what Rømer needed and didn't need:

- He needed only **relative** orbital geometry (Earth's phase angle), which Kepler's laws gave him.
- He did **not** need the absolute size of Earth's orbit to establish that light is finite.

Finiteness comes free from the correlation. Converting to a *speed* requires the AU in kilometres — a separate, much harder problem. Rømer himself only ever quoted a **time**: about 22 minutes for light to cross Earth's orbital diameter. (Modern value: 16.7 minutes. He was ~30% high.)

Huygens did the conversion, using Cassini's 1672 Mars-parallax value for the AU, and got something around 220,000 km/s — roughly 26% low, the error inherited almost entirely from the 22-minute figure.

### Why it wasn't immediately accepted

Cassini had actually floated the light-time idea himself in 1675, then backed away. The objection was reasonable: the same effect should show up in Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, and 17th-century timings of those were too poor to confirm it cleanly. Io's eclipses are the sharpest because Io is closest and its shadow entry is quickest. Also, Io's orbital period itself has real physical variations (we now know these come from the Laplace resonance with Europa and Ganymede), which muddied the residuals.

So for ~50 years the result was respected but contested.

### What settled it

**Bradley, 1728 — stellar aberration.** Completely independent phenomenon: because Earth moves at ~30 km/s, starlight appears tilted by an angle $\tan\theta = v/c$. Bradley measured $\theta \approx 20.5''$, giving $v/c \approx 1/10{,}000$, so $c \approx 301{,}000$ km/s — within 0.4% of modern. Two unrelated methods agreeing killed the debate.

Then terrestrial confirmation: Fizeau's toothed wheel (1849, ~315,000 km/s) and Foucault's rotating mirror (1862, ~298,000 km/s), which removed astronomy from the loop entirely.

Since 1983 the logic inverted: $c \equiv 299{,}792{,}458$ m/s **by definition**, and the metre is derived from it. You can no longer measure $c$; you measure lengths.

### The connection back to your original point

Rømer's effect is not a historical curiosity — it's still the correction, and still bears his name. In pulsar timing, the largest term you subtract from arrival times is the **Rømer delay**:

$$\Delta_{R} = -\frac{\vec{r}_{\text{obs}} \cdot \hat{n}}{c}$$

where $\vec{r}_{\text{obs}}$ is the observatory's position relative to the solar system barycentre and $\hat{n}$ points at the source. Amplitude: up to ~500 seconds. Exactly Rømer's geometry, with a pulsar substituted for Io.

Planetary ephemerides (JPL DE series) do the same thing — they solve the light-time equation iteratively, because to know where Jupiter *was* when the light left, you need to know how far away it was, which depends on where it was.

Worth noting the inversion: Rømer used known geometry to find unknown $c$. Modern astronomers use known $c$ to find unknown geometry — light-time is now a *ranging* tool (spacecraft Doppler/range, lunar laser ranging at ~mm precision).

[^r-mer-delay]: The light-travel-time correction applied to astronomical arrival times to account for the observer's changing distance from a source, computed as the projection of the observer's position relative to the solar system barycentre onto the line of sight, divided by c. Named for Ole Rømer, who inferred light's finite speed in 1676 from exactly this effect in the eclipse timings of Jupiter's moon Io; in pulsar timing it is the largest such term, reaching roughly 500 seconds.
