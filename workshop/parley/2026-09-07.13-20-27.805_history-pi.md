💬: tell me how we discovered Pi, what was it used for. who did the initial discovery. I guess it's about making wheel? 

🤖:[claude-opus-5*]

### Short answer to your guess

Probably not wheels. Wheels existed from roughly 3500 BCE, long before any attested value of π — but you don't need π to build a wheel. If you want to know how far a wheel travels, you can just wrap a cord around it and measure. π becomes necessary only when you *cannot* measure the thing directly, or when you must convert between a length and an area/volume.

That's the real driver: **grain**. How much barley fits in a cylindrical silo of diameter 9 cubits? You can measure the diameter with a rod, but you cannot measure volume without the constant.

### There is no discoverer

π isn't a thing someone found; it's a consequence of the fact that **all circles are similar**. The genuine intellectual discovery is that circumference/diameter is the *same number for every circle* — and that the *area* ratio (area/r²) happens to be that same number, which is not obvious at all.

- Euclid[^liu-hui] (*Elements* XII.2, ~300 BCE) proved circles are proportional to the squares on their diameters — constancy established, value not computed.
- Archimedes (*Measurement of a Circle*, ~250 BCE) proved area = ½ × radius × circumference, welding the two constants into one.

The Rhind Papyrus problem is literally about a circular field of diameter 9: treat it as a square of side 8. Egyptian use was land area and granary volume — certainly agricultural bookkeeping and taxation, not geometry for its own sake.

### What Archimedes actually did

This is the methodological leap worth dwelling on. Earlier values were *measured or guessed approximations*. Archimedes gave an **algorithm with error bounds**:

- Inscribe and circumscribe regular polygons around a circle.
- The circle's perimeter is trapped strictly between them.
- Double the sides: 6 → 12 → 24 → 48 → 96.
- At 96 sides, the trap is tight enough to give 22/7 as an upper bound.

He didn't claim to know π. He proved it lives in a specific interval. That distinction — approximation *with a certificate* — is arguably more important than the number.

### Later, briefly

- **Symbol π**: William Jones, 1706; popularised by Euler from 1737. From Greek *περιφέρεια* (periphery).
- **Lambert, 1761**: π is irrational — no fraction ever equals it.
- **Lindemann, 1882**: π is transcendental — which finally killed *squaring the circle*, a problem open for ~2000 years.

### The question behind your question

If you're asking "did practical need drive the mathematics?" — the honest answer is *initially yes, then decisively no*. Babylonian and Egyptian π was engineering. From Archimedes onward, the precision massively exceeded any practical requirement: 39 digits suffice to compute the circumference of the observable universe to within a hydrogen atom. Everything past that was pursued because π turned out to be a knot where geometry, analysis, and number theory all tie together.
