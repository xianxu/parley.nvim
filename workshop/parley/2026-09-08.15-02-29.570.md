### It's kinematics, not geometry

The mean synodic period needs no geometry at all — only two angular rates:

$$\omega_{\rm syn} = \omega_\oplus - \omega_{\rm J} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{S} = \frac{1}{P_\oplus} - \frac{1}{P_{\rm J}}$$

With $P_\oplus = 365.256$ d and $P_{\rm J} = 4332.59$ d:

$$\frac{1}{S} = 0.00273790 - 0.00023079 = 0.00250711 \;\Rightarrow\; S = 398.87\ \text{d}$$

It is a **beat frequency**. Same math as two clock hands realigning, or two sine waves of nearby frequency. Because Jupiter is slow, $S$ is only slightly longer than Earth's year — Earth laps Jupiter roughly every 13 months.

### Where the ellipses actually enter

Your instinct that eccentricity should matter is right, but it matters in a *different place* than you'd guess:

- **Not in the mean period.** $S = 398.87$ d uses *mean motions*, which are defined by semi-major axes alone (Kepler III). Eccentricity is invisible here — that's a real theorem, not an approximation.
- **In the instantaneous rate.** By Kepler II, both planets speed up at perihelion. So *successive* oppositions are not spaced exactly 398.87 d apart; the interval wobbles by a few days around the mean. Earth's $e = 0.0167$ contributes a little, Jupiter's $e = 0.0489$ more. I'm moderately confident the observed spread is roughly ±2–3 days, but I don't recall the exact bounds.
- **In the distance — which is what Rømer cared about.** Circular approximation:

$$d(t) = \sqrt{a_\oplus^2 + a_{\rm J}^2 - 2\,a_\oplus a_{\rm J}\cos\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi t}{S}\right)}$$

ranging 4.20 → 6.20 AU. The 2 AU swing is exactly Earth's orbital diameter, and it's why the light-time residual has amplitude ~16.7 min. Add ellipticity and the *opposition* distance itself varies between about 3.95 and 4.5 AU depending on where Jupiter sits in its own orbit — which is why some Jupiter oppositions are notably brighter than others.

  ```python
  import numpy as np
  P_E, P_J = 365.256, 4332.589
  S = 1/(1/P_E - 1/P_J)
  t = np.linspace(0, S, 5)
  d = np.sqrt(1.0**2 + 5.204**2 - 2*1.0*5.204*np.cos(2*np.pi*t/S))
  print(f"S = {S:.2f} d")
  print(np.round(d, 3))   # 4.204 ... 6.204 ... 4.204
  ```

### The consequence worth noticing

$S - P_\oplus \approx 34$ days. So opposition drifts about a month later each year, and Jupiter creeps ~30° eastward through the zodiac annually — one constellation per year, twelve years per circuit. That regularity is why Chinese astronomy called Jupiter 岁星 ("year star") and built a 12-year counting cycle on it. Certainly the same 399-day number, seen from a calendar-maker's angle instead of a light-speed measurer's.

Also: retrograde motion is locked to this cycle. Jupiter appears to reverse for ~120 days centred on each opposition — once per synodic period, by construction.
