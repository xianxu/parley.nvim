🤖:[ToolOpus*]

You've spotted the right thing to push on. The FL *ratio* rule is folklore — a proxy. But there are two real physical constraints underneath it, and they both punish short focal lengths.

### Constraint 1: angular precision

The scale relation:

  ```
  scale ["/px] = 206.265 × pixel_size[µm] / FL[mm]
  ```

Guide precision in arcsec = `scale × centroid_precision_in_px`. Since centroid precision is roughly a fixed *fraction of a pixel*, shorter FL directly multiplies your error.

| guide FL (ASI120, 3.75µm) | scale | error @ 0.15px centroid |
|---|---|---|
| 200mm | 3.9"/px | 0.58" |
| 120mm (30F4) | 6.45"/px | 0.97" |
| 50mm | 15.5"/px | 2.3" |
| 24mm | 32"/px | 4.8" |

Target: comfortably under one main-camera pixel (1.81"). So 120mm passes, 50mm is marginal, 24mm fails outright.

But notice — 50mm isn't *catastrophic* by this math. If this were the only constraint, a fast 50mm would be defensible. It isn't.

### Constraint 2: sampling — this is the one that kills it

Centroiding needs the star's light spread across **≥2 pixels**, ideally 2–4. The algorithm infers subpixel position from the *ratio* of intensities between adjacent pixels. If the star lands entirely inside one pixel, there is no ratio — the centroid quantizes to the pixel center and your 0.15px precision collapses to ~0.5px.

Here's the trap: star size on the sensor is set by **aperture** (diffraction + seeing), which is *angular*, while pixel scale is set by focal length. Shorten the FL and the star shrinks in pixels while staying the same in arcsec.

Diffraction FWHM ≈ 1.03λ/D:
- 30mm aperture → **~3.9"**
- 28mm aperture (a 50mm f/1.8) → **~4.2"**

Essentially identical. Now convert to pixels:
- 30F4 @ 120mm: 3.9" / 6.45"/px = **0.6px** from diffraction alone
- 50mm lens: 4.2" / 15.5"/px = **0.27px**

The 50mm is hopelessly undersampled.

### Now — finishing the sampling argument

I left off at the uncomfortable part, so let me state it plainly rather than paper over it.

**A 30mm guide scope is undersampled, and 150mm doesn't fully fix it.**

Star FWHM through a 30mm aperture:
- Diffraction: 1.03λ/D ≈ 3.9"
- Bay Area seeing: ~2–3"
- Quadrature sum: ≈ 4.6"

At 5.16"/px that's **0.9px**. Nyquist wants ≥2px. So the raw centroid really is degraded — the folklore "0.15px precision" is optimistic here.

**What rescues it — and this is the actual answer to your question:**

Autoguiding is a *closed loop with a low-pass filter*, not a measurement system. Three things compound:

1. **Deliberate defocus.** Standard practice, and counterintuitive to a photographer. Slightly defocusing spreads the star to 2–3px and *improves* centroid precision. You are not making a picture; you are making a measurable centroid.
2. **Temporal averaging.** Each guide frame is 1–3s. Centroid noise is largely random frame-to-frame, so it averages as ~1/√N.
3. **Aggressiveness < 100%** (typically 60–80%). The loop deliberately under-corrects, attenuating injected noise. Random centroid error does *not* propagate 1:1 into mount motion — that's the whole reason "chasing the seeing" is a solvable problem rather than a fatal one.

**Why this breaks at 50mm and collapses at 24mm:**

Mechanism 1 (defocus) has a floor — you can't defocus past a few px without losing SNR on faint stars. Mechanisms 2 and 3 only suppress *random* error. They do nothing for **systematic** error: differential flexure, and the fact that at 32"/px a genuine 5" drift is 0.15px — indistinguishable from noise. The loop cannot correct what it cannot see above its own noise floor.

That's the real principle. Not a FL ratio. **The guide system must resolve the errors it's meant to correct, at a scale where they exceed the centroid noise floor.** 150mm/5"/px sees a 3" drift as 0.6px — detectable. 24mm sees it as 0.09px — invisible.

### Consequence for you

Given ~0.9px sampling, expect to **defocus intentionally** during guide-star selection. If you focus the guide scope sharply out of photographer's instinct, your RMS will be worse, not better. This is probably the single most common first-night mistake for people coming from terrestrial photography.

### Physical size

Dimensions 155 × 44 × 73 mm, 348g and 155mm long. Add an ASI120MM Mini on the back (~60mm) and you have a **~215mm long, 350mm³-ish stick weighing ~450g**. Small. The weight is not your problem.

One terminology trap before you order: the dovetail is Synta/Vixen *finder* style — that's the small finder-shoe foot, **not** the big Vixen telescope dovetail rail that goes in your GTi saddle. Different things, same brand name. You need a finder *shoe base* to receive it.

### The actual problem

A telescope owner straps tube rings around the OTA and bolts the guide scope on top. You don't have a tube. So the question becomes: **what rigid structure do the lens and the guide scope both attach to?**

This matters more than it sounds, because of the *chain of stiffness*. Whatever bends between the guide sensor and the imaging sensor is differential flexure, and the guide loop will faithfully correct the mount for an error that isn't there — actively making your stars worse.

**Why I'd avoid the hot shoe** (the obvious first idea): the load path is guide scope → hot shoe → magnesium body shell → RF bayonet → lens internals → collar → saddle. That includes the RF mount, which has real rotational play under a cantilevered 450g. Fine at 135mm. At 1.81"/px with 180s subs, probably not.

**The fix:** attach both to the *same dovetail bar*, so the path is guide scope → bar → lens foot. Two joints, both metal-on-metal.

### Hard prerequisite: the lens tripod collar

The RF 100-500 must ride on its collar foot, not the camera body's 1/4"-20 socket. A 2.3kg system cantilevered off a camera baseplate will sag visibly.

I'm **not certain** whether Canon includes Tripod Mount Ring D (WII) with the RF 100-500 in the US market — I believe it's a separate ~$200 accessory. Check your box. If you don't have it, that purchase precedes the guide scope in priority.

Also note the collar foot is **not Arca-Swiss natively** (moderate confidence) — it's a flat foot with 1/4"-20 and 3/8" holes, which actually helps you here: it bolts straight through a dovetail bar's slot.

### Two workable configurations

**A — In-line, guide scope forward** (simplest, my recommendation to start)

  ```
  [ ==== R5 body ==== | lens collar foot | ---- lens barrel ---- ]
  [        30F5 + cam ]
  [============ Vixen dovetail bar, ~250-300mm ==============]
              ^ into GTi saddle
  ```

Guide scope sits *beside/below the camera body end*, lens foot bolts to the bar mid-span. Keeps mass near the DEC axis and needs no extra hardware beyond a finder shoe screwed to the bar.

Watch: the 30F5 is 73mm tall including foot. Check it clears the GTi's RA housing during a meridian-adjacent pointing.

**B — Side-by-side plate** (stiffer, more $, worse balance)

A flat plate ~200 × 90mm with the guide scope offset laterally. Better clearance, but a 450g lateral offset creates a **DEC imbalance you must counter** — usually by sliding the lens the other way, which then throws off your framing-neutral position.

Start with A. Move to B only if you measure a clearance problem.

### Balance procedure — and the gotcha

The GTi is a German equatorial, so you balance **two axes**, in this order:

1. **DEC first.** Lock RA, unlock DEC, rotate the payload to horizontal. Slide the dovetail bar fore/aft in the saddle until it stays put at any angle.
2. **RA second.** Lock DEC, unlock RA (counterweight shaft horizontal). Slide the counterweight until balanced.
3. Some people deliberately leave RA **slightly east-heavy** so the worm gear always pushes against one tooth face, eliminating backlash. Worth trying once you have guiding data to compare.

**The gotcha that will bite you:** the RF 100-500 is an *externally extending* zoom. Going 100 → 500mm pushes the front element forward several centimetres and shifts the center of mass substantially. **Balance at 500mm, zoom locked, with the guide scope, camera, and all cables attached** — i.e., the exact shooting configuration. If you balance at 100mm and then zoom, you've re-introduced the imbalance you just removed.

Same logic for the ASIAIR itself and its cable loom — mount the ASIAIR on the bar (ZWO sells a dovetail-clamp holder) so its weight is part of the balanced system, not hanging off the tripod.

### Why the 180° flip backfires

Your proposal reverses the load path:

| | load path | weakest joint |
|---|---|---|
| **Collar down (correct)** | bar → collar foot → lens barrel; camera hangs off the back | none — collar is steel-on-alloy, designed for this |
| **Collar up (your idea)** | bar → R5 baseplate → magnesium shell → **RF bayonet** → lens (1.5kg cantilevered forward) | RF bayonet, in torsion |

The bayonet is a bearing surface with deliberate clearance so lenses can be twisted on and off. It has microscopic rotational play. Hanging 1.5kg of extending zoom off it, at 1.81"/px, is asking for exactly the flexure you're buying a guide scope to eliminate.

There's a second, subtler failure: a rotated collar is held by a **clamp screw on a smooth ring**. That's a friction joint. Friction joints creep under sustained load and temperature change — slowly, monotonically, over exactly the 2-3 hour timescale of an imaging session. Silent, undiagnosable drift.

So: **collar foot down, always.** The camera's 1/4"-20 socket stays unused.

### The joint you actually need to buy

Two options, depending on the 30F5 foot question I flagged:

**If the 30F5 foot has a 1/4"-20 hole** — screw it straight into the bar. Done, zero extra parts.

**If it's Synta finder-shoe only** — you need a *finder shoe base with a 1/4" mounting hole* (~$15-25, sold by Svbony/Astromania/ZWO). Screws into the bar slot; the 30F5's foot slides into it.

For the bar itself: get a **slotted Vixen bar, 250-300mm**, ideally one with 1/4"-20 holes at intervals rather than a single continuous slot — you want the lens foot repeatable, since you'll rebalance every session.

### The relief you may not have realized

**The guide scope does not need to be bore-sighted.** Not even close.

Your guide FOV is 1.8° × 1.4° (ASI120) and it only needs *some* star in it — not a star in your target frame. Point it within a couple of degrees of the main lens and you're fine. Getting it "parallel by eye" is genuinely sufficient.

This is why the side-offset in the top view is harmless, and why you shouldn't spend money on adjustable-alignment guide rings. Rigidity is everything; alignment is nearly free.

### Why front and back both fail

**Front-mounted:** the 100-500 at 500mm has a hood/barrel roughly 90-100mm in diameter. If the guide scope sits inline along the bar's axis, it's physically in the shadow of that barrel — either literally blocked, or so close it vignettes. Not viable.

**Back-mounted:** the space between the collar foot (where the bar attaches, near the lens's balance point) and the camera's rear LCD is maybe 80-100mm — smaller than the 30F5's own 155mm length. It doesn't fit, and this space is also where you need slide range for DEC balancing.

### The real answer: sideways, on a wide plate

You had it — "mount on both sides of the dovetail" is the right instinct, but the part that has to change is the bar itself. A standard Vixen bar is a **narrow rail** (~35-45mm wide) — just enough for one foot. You need a **wide plate** instead: same dovetail foot underneath to clamp into the GTi saddle, but a broad top surface with mounting points on two parallel tracks.

  ```
  TOP VIEW

  ┌───────────────────────────────────────┐
  │   [30F5 + ASI120]  ← offset track      │
  │                                        │
  │   [R5]======[RF 100-500]=====>         │  ← centerline track
  └───────────────────────────────────────┘
              |
       [Vixen dovetail foot underneath]
              |
          [GTi saddle]
  ```

**Clearance math:** optical axis sits roughly 40-50mm above the collar foot's mounting face on a big lens like this. Hood radius ~50mm. Guide scope tube radius ~20mm. So you want centerline-to-centerline lateral offset of roughly **80-90mm**, meaning a plate around **160-180mm wide** total. That clears the hood with margin. Height-match isn't critical — recall the guide scope doesn't need precise alignment, just no obstruction.

### Two concrete ways to build this

1. **Purpose-made astro plate** — sold as "universal dovetail plate" or "dual saddle plate" by Losmandy/ADM/William Optics-type vendors, ~150-200mm wide, pre-drilled 1/4"-20 rows, Vixen foot built in. ~$40-60.
2. **Camera-rigging cheese plate** — since you're a photographer, you may already know this world: a SmallRig-style aluminum cheese plate (grid of 1/4"-20 holes, ~$20-30) bolted onto a separate Vixen dovetail foot (~$15-20) underneath. Lens collar goes on the centerline holes, a small finder-shoe base for the 30F5 goes on an offset hole, raised on a short block/riser if needed to clear the hood height. More flexible, cheaper, and probably faster for you to source given existing familiarity.

I'd lean toward option 2 for you specifically — it's the kind of modular hardware a photographer already has intuition for, versus astro-specific parts that are a new vocabulary.

**One thing I'm not certain of:** exact optical-axis height for the RF 100-500's collar — I estimated ~40-50mm from general big-lens proportions, not a spec sheet. Worth a quick physical check with a ruler once the collar's in hand, before buying/drilling the offset track position.
