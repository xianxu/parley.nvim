### 1. "Babylonian" — who and what

Old Babylonian scribes in southern Mesopotamia, roughly 1900–1600 BCE, writing cuneiform on clay in base-60. Two tablets carry the π content:

**YBC 7302** (Yale collection) — a circle inscribed with three numbers: `3` on the outside, `9` beside it, `45` in the middle. Read as: circumference 3, its square 9, area 0;45 (= ¾). So the rule is

$$A = \frac{C^2}{12}$$

Since $A = C^2/(4\pi)$ exactly, this rule *is* π = 3. But notice what they did: **area computed from circumference, not from radius.** That's the fingerprint of a rope-and-peg surveyor. Circumference is the thing you can physically wrap a cord around; the radius of a marked-out field is harder to get at. This is the strongest evidence that π entered mathematics through *land and grain*, not through wheels.

**The Susa tablet** (excavated in Iran, 1930s) is the more interesting one. It gives the ratio of the perimeter of a regular hexagon to the circumference of the circle around it as 0;57,36 sexagesimal = 57/60 + 36/3600 = **0.96**. Work it out: hexagon perimeter is $6r$, circle is $2\pi r$, so $3/\pi = 0.96$, giving

$$\pi = 25/8 = 3.125$$

That is a *polygon comparison*. A Babylonian scribe, ~1400 years before Archimedes, was already comparing an inscribed hexagon to its circle. He just stopped at six sides.

Critical caveat: **nowhere on these tablets is there a constant named, isolated, or reasoned about.** There is no "π." There are baked procedures — "square the circumference, take a twelfth" — with no statement that the twelfth is universal or where it came from. Whether the scribes understood 25/8 as an *approximation* to something exact, or just as a better number, is genuinely unknown. My confidence that they had the concept "there exists a fixed ratio, and our value is merely near it" is low, maybe 25%.

### 2. Archimedes — the first real mathematics

Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287–212 BCE). The relevant work is a short treatise, *Measurement of a Circle*, only three propositions long. It is the hinge of this whole story.

**Proposition 1** is the conceptual breakthrough, and it's usually skipped over in favour of the number. He proved:

$$A = \tfrac{1}{2} \, r \, C$$

The area of a circle equals that of a right triangle with legs $r$ and $C$. Why this matters: before Archimedes there were *two independent empirical constants* floating around — one relating circumference to diameter, another relating area to the square of the radius. Nothing obvious says they're the same number. Proposition 1 collapses them into one. After this, "π" is a single object.

The proof method is **exhaustion with double reductio**: assume the area is greater than the triangle, derive a contradiction using inscribed polygons; assume it's less, contradiction using circumscribed polygons; therefore equal. No limits, no infinity — Greek mathematics was rigorously finite, and this was the workaround.

**Proposition 3** is the famous number. Start with a hexagon, double repeatedly: 6 → 12 → 24 → 48 → 96 sides. Compute the perimeter of the inscribed 96-gon (too small) and the circumscribed 96-gon (too big). Result:

$$3\tfrac{10}{71} < \pi < 3\tfrac{1}{7} \qquad (3.1408\ldots < \pi < 3.1428\ldots)$$

Three things make this categorically different from everything before it:

- **It's an algorithm, not a value.** Want more digits? Keep doubling. The procedure has no natural stopping point. Every later polygon computation for 1800 years — Liu Hui, Zu Chongzhi, al-Kāshī, Ludolph van Ceulen — is running Archimedes' loop harder.
- **It brackets from both sides.** You don't just get an estimate, you get a proof that the truth lies in a stated interval. This is the birth of numerical error bounds.
- **He knew π was neither of his fractions.** 22/7 is an *upper bound* he explicitly proved, not a value he believed. The modern schoolroom habit of teaching "π = 22/7" inverts his actual achievement.

The hidden labour: he had no decimals, no algebra, no trigonometry. Doubling a polygon requires square roots, computed by hand in sexagesimal. He asserts $\frac{265}{153} < \sqrt{3} < \frac{1351}{780}$ with **no explanation whatsoever** — accurate to six decimal places. How he got those is still argued over (probably a Heron-style iteration). It's one of the more tantalising silences in ancient mathematics.

Worth knowing: Archimedes himself considered his best result the sphere-to-cylinder ratio of 2:3, and asked for it on his tomb — Cicero found the grave overgrown ~137 years later and had it restored. And in the *Method*, recovered from a scraped-and-overwritten prayer book (the Archimedes Palimpsest, re-read with X-rays only in the 1990s), he explains that he *found* results by mentally slicing shapes into infinitely many strips and balancing them on a lever, then went back and proved them rigorously by exhaustion. That's essentially integral calculus used as a discovery heuristic, 1900 years early, and he deliberately kept it out of his published proofs because he didn't consider it valid.

He was killed in 212 BCE at the fall of Syracuse to Rome, by a soldier, against orders. "Do not disturb my circles" is almost certainly a later embellishment.

### 3. Liu Hui — the independent line

Liu Hui (c. 225–295 CE), of the state of Wei during the Three Kingdoms period. Essentially nothing is known of his life — no birth date, no office, no biography. What survives is his work.

In 263 CE he wrote a **commentary on the *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*** (九章算術), the canonical Chinese mathematical text, itself a compilation of problems and recipes with no proofs. Liu Hui's role was to supply what the *Nine Chapters* lacked: **justification**. He is, in effect, China's answer to the question "why should these recipes be true?"

His π work is the 割圓術, **"circle-cutting method."** Structurally like Archimedes — inscribed regular polygons, sides doubled — but with distinct features:

- He explicitly attacked the inherited value 3, pointing out it is the ratio of the *hexagon's* perimeter to the diameter, not the circle's. (The same hexagon that trapped the Babylonians. He named the trap.)
- He used **only inscribed polygons**, yet still got two-sided bounds, via the inequality

  $$S_{2n} < S < S_{2n} + (S_{2n} - S_n)$$

  The true area exceeds the $2n$-gon by less than the last gain did. This is more economical than Archimedes' approach — no circumscribed figures needed at all — and it's arguably the cleverer piece of reasoning.
- At 96 and 192 sides he obtained 3.14, recorded as **157/50** (the "Hui rate"). A later passage credits him with 3072 sides and **3.1416** (3927/1250).
