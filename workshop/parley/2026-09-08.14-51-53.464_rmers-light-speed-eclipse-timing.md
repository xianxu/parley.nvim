### What is actually timed

A single instant, not a duration. Rømer recorded the moment of **immersion** (Io winks out entering Jupiter's shadow) *or* **emersion** (it reappears) — one clock reading, to the nearest few seconds, against a pendulum clock calibrated on stellar transits.

He could rarely get both from the same eclipse, for a geometric reason: Jupiter's shadow points away from the Sun, so when Earth is on one side of opposition the immersion happens off Jupiter's visible limb while the emersion occurs behind Jupiter's disk (occulted, invisible), and after opposition it flips. Near opposition the shadow hides behind the planet entirely and neither event is clean. So in practice you observe immersions for months, then emersions for months.

### Your objection is exactly right — and it's the crux

If you timed one eclipse's *duration* (~2.3 hours for Io), the light-time delay would apply almost equally to both ends and cancel. Almost — Earth moves ~30 km/s, so over 2.3 hours the distance changes by ~250,000 km, i.e. **under one second** of differential delay. Invisible to a 1676 clock.

The absolute delay is even worse: it is completely unobservable. You cannot tell that the eclipse "really" happened 40 minutes ago without already knowing the distance and $c$. **Only *changes* in delay are observable.**

### So what Rømer really used: Io as a clock, not as an event

Io's period is 42.456 hours — a metronome. Rømer's data are the *intervals between successive eclipses*, compared against a constant assumed period.

- When Earth recedes from Jupiter, each successive eclipse arrives **late** by $\Delta D / c$, where $\Delta D$ is the distance added during one Io period.
- Near quadrature, Earth's full 30 km/s is along the line of sight: $30 \times 153{,}000\,\text{s} \approx 4.6$ million km $\to$ **~15 seconds late** per eclipse.
- Accumulate over ~112 eclipses (half a synodic period, ~200 days) and the phase error piles up to the full $2\,\text{AU}/c \approx 16.7$ minutes.

That accumulation is the whole trick. The per-orbit signal (10–15 s) sits at the edge of 17th-century timing noise; the *sum* over months is 1000 s and unmistakable.

### The modern reading

This is literally an integrated Doppler measurement:

$$P_\text{obs} = P_\text{true}\left(1 + \frac{v_r}{c}\right), \qquad \sum \left(P_\text{obs} - P_\text{true}\right) = \frac{\Delta D}{c}$$

Rømer was measuring the radial velocity of Earth relative to Jupiter, expressed in units of $c$ — 170 years before Doppler wrote it down. And note what the observation gives you unaided: $\Delta D / c$ in seconds. The *finiteness* is proven right there, from geometry alone. Turning it into 220,000 km/s required Huygens to plug in a value for the AU — which is why Rømer himself only ever published the delay ("22 minutes"), never a speed.
