### Your dead end is the real historical problem

Your formula is right: `π = lim N→∞ N·sin(180°/N)`. But notice you can't evaluate it, and the reason is circular — every way you know to compute `sin` (calculator, Taylor series in radians) already presupposes π. Archimedes' genius was escaping exactly this trap: he never computed a sine, [he computed **side lengths by recursion using only Pythagoras and square roots**.]

### Archimedes (c. 250 BC): doubling the polygon

Take a unit circle. Let `s_n` be the side length of the inscribed regular n-gon. The half-angle construction (drop a perpendicular from the center to a side, apply Pythagoras twice) gives, with no trigonometry:

`s_2n = √(2 − √(4 − s_n²))`

Start with the hexagon, where `s_6 = 1` exactly (the side of an inscribed hexagon equals the radius — a fact needing no π). Then perimeter `p_n = n·s_n → 2π`.

Archimedes did this by hand for 6 → 12 → 24 → 48 → 96 sides, **both inscribed and circumscribed**, so he got a rigorous *interval*, not an estimate:

`223/71 < π < 22/7`  (i.e. 3.1408 < π < 3.1429)

The hard part was that each step needs a square root, and he had no decimals — he had to produce provable rational bounds like `265/153 < √3 < 1351/780`. Nobody knows how he found those; it's an open historical question.

### The polygon era (and its ceiling)

| Who | When | Result | Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liu Hui (China) | c. 263 | 3.1416 | 3072-gon, plus a clever extrapolation |
| Zu Chongzhi | c. 480 | 355/113, 7 digits | ~24576-gon |
| Al-Kāshī | 1424 | 16 digits | 3·2²⁸-gon |
| Ludolph van Ceulen | 1596–1610 | 35 digits | 2⁶²-gon, his life's work |

That table *is* the ceiling. Polygon methods converge linearly — doubling the sides buys you about 0.6 of a decimal digit. Van Ceulen spent decades for 35 digits; the digits were carved on his tombstone.

### Viète (1593): your limit in closed form

If you unroll Archimedes' doubling algebraically instead of numerically, you get the first infinite expression for π in Western mathematics:

`2/π = (√2/2) · (√(2+√2)/2) · (√(2+√(2+√2))/2) · …`

This is literally `cos(π/4)·cos(π/8)·cos(π/16)…`. So your intuition — polygons in the limit — *does* terminate in a formula. It's just still slow.

### The series revolution (1400s–1700s)

The breakthrough was abandoning geometry for **analysis**. Madhava in Kerala (c. 1400), and independently Gregory/Leibniz in Europe:

`arctan x = x − x³/3 + x⁵/5 − …`

At `x = 1` this gives `π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − …` — beautiful and useless (you need ~10¹⁰ terms for 10 digits). The trick is to evaluate arctan at *small* x, where convergence is fast. Machin, 1706:

`π/4 = 4·arctan(1/5) − arctan(1/239)`

100 digits. Machin-like formulas dominated for **250 years**, up to Shanks' 707 digits in 1873 (of which 527 were correct — the error stood undetected for 71 years).

### Modern era

- **Gauss–Legendre / AGM** (1976, used by Kanada): *quadratic* convergence — each iteration doubles the digit count. ~25 iterations gives 45 million digits.
- **Ramanujan (1914) → Chudnovsky (1988)**: series adding ~14 digits *per term*. Still the record-holder's tool; current records are in the hundreds of trillions of digits.
- **BBP formula** (1995): computes the nth hexadecimal digit of π **without computing the preceding ones** — genuinely shocking when discovered.

### Back to your circularity

The modern resolution: define `sin` and `cos` by their power series (pure analysis, no geometry), prove `cos` has a smallest positive zero, and **define** π as twice that zero. Then `lim N·sin(π/N) = π` follows from `sin x / x → 1`, and "circumference/diameter = π" becomes a *theorem* rather than a definition. Lindemann (1882) then proved π transcendental, killing squaring the circle.

### Runnable Archimedes

Note the naive recursion suffers catastrophic cancellation (`2 − √(4−s²)` subtracts nearly-equal numbers). Rationalizing fixes it:

  ```python
  # archimedes.py
  from math import sqrt

  def pi_archimedes(steps, stable=True):
      n, s = 6, 1.0          # inscribed hexagon: side == radius
      out = []
      for _ in range(steps):
          if stable:
              s = s / sqrt(2 + sqrt(4 - s*s))   # rationalized
          else:
              s = sqrt(2 - sqrt(4 - s*s))       # naive, loses precision
          n *= 2
          out.append((n, n * s / 2))            # perimeter/2 -> pi
      return out

  if __name__ == "__main__":
      print("stable:")
      for n, p in pi_archimedes(28):
          print(f"{n:>12} {p:.15f}")
      print("\nnaive (watch it collapse):")
      for n, p in pi_archimedes(28, stable=False):
          print(f"{n:>12} {p:.15f}")
  ```

Run it:

  ```
  python3 archimedes.py
  ```

You should see the stable version reach 3.141592653589793 and stay; the naive one drifts and then degenerates to 0 around n ≈ 10⁸. Archimedes stopped at n = 96, where this prints 3.14103195089051 — matching his lower bound 223/71.

And a Machin check for contrast — 4 terms of each arctan already beats van Ceulen's lifetime work at 96 sides:

  ```python
  from decimal import Decimal, getcontext

  def arctan_inv(x, terms):     # arctan(1/x)
      total, power = Decimal(0), Decimal(1) / Decimal(x)
      xsq = Decimal(x) * Decimal(x)
      for k in range(terms):
          total += (power / (2*k + 1)) * (-1 if k % 2 else 1)
          power /= xsq
      return total

  getcontext().prec = 60
  pi = 4 * (4*arctan_inv(5, 50) - arctan_inv(239, 20))
  print(pi)
  ```

### Sharper: they *did* have trigonometry — and it was built by that exact recursion

The Greeks had a trig function: the **chord**, `crd θ` = the straight-line distance between two points on a circle subtending angle θ. In modern terms, for radius R:

`crd θ = 2R·sin(θ/2)`

Hipparchus (c. 150 BC) tabulated chords; Ptolemy's *Almagest* (c. 150 AD) has a full table in half-degree steps, R = 60, three sexagesimal places. So sine tables existed for ~1800 years before "sine" was defined analytically.

### The punchline

How did Ptolemy *build* the table? Two facts, plus a half-angle step:

- Thales/Pythagoras: `crd²θ + crd²(180° − θ) = 4R²`
- Half-chord: `crd(θ/2) = √(2R² − R·crd(180° − θ))`

Set R = 1 and substitute the first into the second:

`crd(θ/2) = √(2 − √(4 − crd²θ))`

That is **character-for-character Archimedes' polygon recursion**. It isn't that they were lucky to lack sin/cos — it's that the recursion is *logically prior* to the sine table. Any π-free construction of trig values has to bottom out here.

### What made the table possible at all

Ptolemy needed seed values, and he got them from constructible polygons: `crd 60° = R` (hexagon), `crd 90° = R√2`, `crd 72°` and `crd 36°` from the pentagon (golden ratio), then Ptolemy's theorem on cyclic quadrilaterals gives chord addition/subtraction. Halving fills in the rest.

But he could never reach `crd 1°` exactly — that needs trisecting 3°, which is impossible with these tools. He *bounded* it by inequality and interpolated. A visible crack in the method, 2000 years before Wantzel explained why.

### The deep version of your remark

Your instinct is right in a way you may not have intended. Everything reachable by hexagon-doubling lives in the field of **nested square roots** — the ruler-and-compass constructible numbers. The recursion is closed under exactly the operations Greek geometry could legitimize.

And that's also the method's death sentence: π is transcendental, so it is *not* in that field. No polygon in the sequence ever equals π — only the limit does. The Greeks were computing inside a system that provably cannot contain the answer, which is precisely why Archimedes framed his result as an **interval** rather than a value. That was intellectual honesty, not modesty.

### Footnote: your calculator

`sin` in a modern libm still can't escape π — argument reduction mod π/2 is the hardest part of the implementation, and needs π to thousands of bits for large inputs. CORDIC hardware dodges it differently, using a precomputed table of `arctan(2⁻ᵏ)` — another set of magic constants someone had to compute first.
