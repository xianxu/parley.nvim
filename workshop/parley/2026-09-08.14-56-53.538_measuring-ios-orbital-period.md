### Short answer: not from one observation — from counting cycles over a long baseline

You can't get a period from a single point in time. The minimum is two events of the same kind, plus knowledge of how many cycles happened in between. That second part is the whole trick.

The observable event is an **eclipse**: Io disappearing into (or emerging from) Jupiter's shadow. That's chosen deliberately — it's a sharp, near-instantaneous on/off transition, far more precisely timeable than trying to eyeball when a moon is at maximum elongation.

### The long-baseline counting argument

Suppose you time one immersion tonight and another one a year later. Io's period is ~42.5 hours (1.769 days), so roughly 206 cycles have elapsed. If you can be *certain* the count is 206 and not 205 or 207, then:

  ```
  P = (t_final - t_initial) / N
  ```

and your error in `P` is your timing error divided by `N`. A crude first estimate (say ±10 minutes from a few consecutive nights) is more than enough to pin the integer `N` unambiguously over a year. Then the year-long baseline shrinks the period error by a factor of ~200. Extend to decades and it shrinks further.

This is a general and very powerful epistemic move — the same structure as a vernier[^laplace-resonance] scale, or phase unwrapping in modern interferometry. **Coarse measurement fixes the integer; long baseline refines the fraction.**

So "regularity" isn't assumed and then never checked. It's the hypothesis that lets you assign the integer count, and it's *tested* every time an intermediate eclipse falls where the extrapolation says it should. Thousands of successful predictions is the evidence.

### The circularity worry — which I think is your real question

If Rømer used Io's regularity to derive that light has finite speed, isn't he assuming exactly the thing that's in question? Why not conclude "Io's orbit is irregular" instead?

The answer is that the residuals have a **very specific signature**, and it doesn't look like anything Io could do on its own:

- The deviation is periodic in the **[🌿:Earth–Jupiter synodic period ](2026-09-08.15-02-29.570.md)(~399 days)** — a quantity that has nothing to do with Io. An intrinsic orbital irregularity would be periodic in Io's period, or in Jupiter's orbit, or in some resonance with the other moons. Not in *Earth's* position.
- The deviation tracks **Earth–Jupiter distance**, and the amplitude matches the geometry: about 16.6 minutes across the full 2 AU diameter of Earth's orbit. (Rømer's own figure was ~22 minutes — he was off, but the *structure* was right.)
- It's an accumulating-*phase* effect, not an accumulating-*energy* effect. Eclipses run late for half the year and then run early again, returning exactly to schedule. Io itself gains and loses nothing.

That's a falsifiable pattern. If eclipses had drifted in a way uncorrelated with Earth's position, the finite-light-speed hypothesis would have died immediately.

There's also a clean way to sidestep the circularity entirely: measure the mean period over a **whole number of synodic periods**. The light-time effect cancels exactly over a full cycle, so the average period comes out unbiased regardless of what light does.

### Is Io's orbit actually regular?

No, not perfectly — and this is worth knowing because it shows the "assumption" is really a statement about *precision*, not about metaphysics.

- Io, Europa, and Ganymede are locked in the **Laplace resonance** (1:2:4). This forces a small libration; I believe the libration period is on the order of 2000+ days with a tiny amplitude, but I'm not confident in the exact figures.
- Tidal dissipation causes slow secular drift in the mean motions.
- Jupiter's oblateness and mutual perturbations add further small terms.

All of these are far below the ~16.6-minute light-time signal, and all were detected *later*, once timing precision improved. So the historical logic holds: regular enough, by a wide margin, for the effect being measured.

### The nice irony

Galileo had proposed using Jupiter's moons as a **universal clock** for determining longitude at sea — precisely *because* they were believed regular and visible from everywhere. Rømer was working inside that program, compiling eclipse tables. The finite speed of light showed up as an *error* in the clock. The tool's anomaly became the discovery.

I'd also note the enabling technology: Huygens' pendulum clock (1650s) gave observatories timekeeping good to seconds per day. Without that, the residuals would have been buried in noise.

### The punchline: Jupiter's distance barely enters the amplitude at all

This is the geometrically pleasing part. Let Jupiter sit at heliocentric distance $R$ and Earth at 1 AU. The Earth–Jupiter distance ranges from

  ```
  opposition:   R - 1 AU   (Earth between Sun and Jupiter)
  conjunction:  R + 1 AU   (Sun between Earth and Jupiter)
  ```

The **range is 2 AU — independent of $R$**. Whether Jupiter is at 5.2 AU or 30 AU, the light-path swing is the diameter of *Earth's* orbit.

So the amplitude measurement gives you $c$ directly:

  ```
  c = 2 AU / 996 s  ≈  3.0 × 10^8 m/s
  ```

with no knowledge of Jupiter's orbit required. Your instinct that Jupiter's distance "should" matter is the natural one, and it's wrong for a nice reason: Jupiter is essentially a fixed direction marker, and Earth does all the moving.

### Where Jupiter's orbit *does* matter — second order

Three places, all corrections rather than essentials:

- **Jupiter moves during the 199 days** from opposition to conjunction (~16° of arc). So the two extremes aren't measured against a static target.
- **Jupiter's eccentricity** ($e \approx 0.048$) makes $R$ vary between 4.95 and 5.46 AU on a 12-year cycle, slowly modulating the baseline.
- **Conjunction is unobservable** — Jupiter is lost in the Sun's glare. You never actually get the endpoint; you fit a curve to partial arcs, and fitting requires the geometry.

So for the crude result: not needed. For a precise fit: needed.

### How we know Jupiter's orbit — the two-layer structure

This is worth separating cleanly, because it's the general architecture of all pre-radar astronomy:

**Layer 1 — shape and relative sizes, from angles alone.** Fully determined, and very precisely, in units of AU. No absolute distance anywhere.

**Layer 2 — the absolute scale.** One single measurement (the AU in metres) converts the whole map to kilometres.

1. Get Jupiter's **sidereal period** from the observed synodic period:
